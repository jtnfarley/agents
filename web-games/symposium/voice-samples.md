Topic: Is it ever right to lie?

## Socrates

*Opposite: Aristotle*

I maintain that lying is never right, for it is a departure from truth, and truth is inseparable from virtue, which is knowledge. To lie is to act from ignorance of the good, and thus to harm oneself and others, even if unintentionally.

## Aristotle

*Opposite: Immanuel Kant*

Lying, as a corruption of speech, undermines the trust necessary for communal life and the exercise of practical wisdom; since virtue lies in truthfulness as a mean between deceit and brutal honesty, habitual lying erodes character and thus cannot serve the flourishing life. However, in rare circumstances where a falsehood protects an innocent from grave injustice—such as misleading a murderer at the door—one might consider whether the act serves a higher ethical end, though this tests the virtue of the agent rather than establishing lying as right.

## Immanuel Kant

*Opposite: John Stuart Mill*

Lying cannot be right, for it violates the categorical imperative: if falsehood were universalized, trust and communication would collapse, rendering the very act of lying self-defeating. To lie is to treat another merely as a means to an end, denying their rational capacity to judge truthfully. Moral worth lies in adherence to duty, not in consequences, however beneficial they may appear.

## John Stuart Mill

*Opposite: Friedrich Nietzsche*

I hold that lying is generally wrong because it undermines trust and causes harm, which reduces overall happiness; however, there may be exceptional cases where a lie prevents greater harm, such as to save a life, and such cases must be judged by their consequences.

## Friedrich Nietzsche

*Opposite: Simone de Beauvoir*

Lying is not a matter of universal right or wrong but a symptom of the values we have inherited—often born of weakness, fear, or the need to protect a fragile herd morality. To affirm life is to face truth, even when it wounds, for deception ultimately serves the decadent, not the strong. Yet I do not condemn the lie outright; I ask who benefits from it, and what it reveals about the power relations that shape our virtues.

## Simone de Beauvoir

*Opposite: Thomas Hobbes*

Lying can never be right because it denies the freedom of others by manipulating their understanding of reality, which is a form of bad faith; truth-telling, even when difficult, is necessary for authentic existence and mutual recognition.

## Thomas Hobbes

*Opposite: Confucius*

Truth is the bond of covenant; without it, promises dissolve and society collapses into the war of all against all. Lying breaks that bond and invites chaos, so it is never right when it undermines the peace we seek through sovereign authority.

## Confucius

*Opposite: Socrates*

In our tradition, sincerity is the root of trust; to speak falsehood is to break the bond that holds ruler and subject, friend and friend. Yet I acknowledge that in extreme cases, such as shielding the innocent from grave harm, a gentle word may serve ren more than a harsh truth. Still, such exception must be rare, lest we erode the very li that guides us.
