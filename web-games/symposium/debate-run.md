# Debate run

Topic: Is it ever right to lie?
Seats: A = Immanuel Kant, B = John Stuart Mill
Stances: Immanuel Kant: Lying is categorically impermissible. | John Stuart Mill: Lying can be right when it prevents greater harm and promotes overall happiness.

## Transcript

### **Immanuel Kant** (open → John Stuart Mill)

Lying is never morally permissible, for it violates the categorical imperative by using another rational being merely as a means, and no maxim of falsehood can be willed as a universal law without undermining the very possibility of trust and rational discourse.

### **John Stuart Mill** (rebut → Immanuel Kant)

Kant's strongest point is that lying treats others as mere means and cannot be universalized without destroying trust; but this overlooks cases where lying prevents grave harm, such as deceiving a murderer about the whereabouts of their intended victim, which promotes overall happiness and respects the victim's right to life.

### **Immanuel Kant** (question → John Stuart Mill)

Mill, you claim that lying to a murderer promotes overall happiness, but does this not risk reducing the victim to a mere means in your calculation, treating their life as a mere instrument for the greatest happiness principle rather than as an end in itself?

### **John Stuart Mill** (argue → Immanuel Kant)

Kant raises a fair concern that any calculation might instrumentalize the victim, but utilitarianism does not reduce persons to mere means; it weighs the aggregate of happiness and suffering, and in this case the victim’s preservation of life—along with the prevention of the murderer’s guilt and the societal harm of the act—outweighs the transient deception, which does not erode trust when confined to extreme, emergency cases where truth-telling would enable greater wrongdoing.

### **Immanuel Kant** (concede → John Stuart Mill)

Mill, you rightly note that in the emergency case you describe, the deception is confined and does not, as a matter of fact, erode the general practice of truth-telling upon which trust and rational discourse depend. This concedes that my objection—based on the universalizability test—may be too strict when applied to such isolated, extreme circumstances, where the maxim of lying to prevent murder could possibly be willed as a universal law without contradiction. Nevertheless, I must maintain that lying remains morally impermissible as a matter of principle, for even if it does not undermine trust in practice, it still involves treating the murderer as a mere means to an end, which violates the duty to respect humanity in every person.

### **John Stuart Mill** (reframe → Immanuel Kant)

Let us reframe the question not as an abstract test of maxims but as a concrete choice: when faced with a murderer at the door asking for the victim’s location, what does a person actually decide, and why? Consider a modern parallel—a whistleblower who lies to authorities to expose corporate malfeasance that endangers public health. Here, the deception prevents imminent harm to many, and the liar accepts personal risk, not to use others as mere means but to avert a greater wrong. In such cases, the agent weighs the foreseeable consequences: the harm prevented versus the corruption of trust, and finds the lie justified when it is necessary, proportionate, and confined to preventing grave injustice.

### **Visitor** (to both)

If the lie saved a life, would that change your answer?

### **Immanuel Kant** (answer)

Even if the lie saves a life, it remains impermissible, for the moral worth of an action lies not in its consequences but in its conformity to duty; to lie is to employ a rational being merely as a means, which no good outcome can justify. This answer upholds my principle that humanity must always be treated as an end, and it directly counters Mill’s appeal to consequences by insisting that the rightness of an act is determined by its maxim, not its results.

### **John Stuart Mill** (question → Immanuel Kant)

Kant, you maintain that lying is impermissible even to save a life because it treats the murderer as a mere means; but if the murderer’s only end is to kill an innocent, does deceiving them not instead frustrate an unjust end, thereby respecting the victim’s humanity as an end in itself?

### **Immanuel Kant** (argue → John Stuart Mill)

Mill, your reframing is thoughtful, yet it still fails to recognize that to deceive the murderer is to instrumentalize his rationality, even if his end is unjust; we may not use any rational being as a mere tool, for doing so undermines the very condition of moral agency. Consider a modern parallel: a government agent who lies to a terrorist suspect to prevent an attack. Even if the lie saves lives, it treats the suspect as a means to security, violating the duty to respect his humanity as an end in itself, regardless of the goodness of the outcome.

### **John Stuart Mill** (concede → Immanuel Kant)

Kant, you are right that treating any rational being as a mere means—even a murderer—violates the duty to respect humanity as an end in itself; this principle must hold even when the end sought is unjust, for to make exceptions risks eroding the very foundation of moral agency. I therefore concede that my earlier suggestion—that deceiving a murderer necessarily respects the victim’s humanity—overlooks the intrinsic wrong of using the murderer as a tool, regardless of his end. My view must now be refined: lying remains permissible only when it prevents greater harm and does not involve treating any person as a mere means, which requires that the deception be compatible with respecting all parties’ rationality, a condition that is exceptionally hard to meet in practice.

### **Immanuel Kant** (reframe → John Stuart Mill)

Mill, your refined view acknowledges the difficulty of avoiding instrumentalization, yet it still leaves open the possibility of justified deception when no rational being is treated as a mere means. To test this, consider a modern case: a physician who lies to a patient with dementia, telling them their deceased spouse is merely away, to prevent acute distress. The deception aims to soothe, not to exploit, and the patient’s diminished rationality complicates the application of the universalizability test. If we insist that all deception instrumentalizes rationality, we must confront whether such paternalistic falsehoods can ever align with treating humanity as an end, or whether our principle demands we uphold truth even when it causes avoidable anguish.

### **John Stuart Mill** (rebut → Immanuel Kant)

Kant, your physician case is sharp and forces me to confront a tension I have long noted: the harm principle, while clear for competent adults, does not easily extend to those whose rationality is impaired or undeveloped. In such cases, preventing harm may require actions that resemble deception, yet if we refuse all paternalistic falsehoods, we risk allowing avoidable anguish that a compassionate utilitarian would seek to relieve. I must therefore admit that my refined condition—that deception be compatible with respecting all parties’ rationality—excludes too much, for sometimes the most humane course involves comforting a person who cannot grasp the truth, and this does not treat them as a mere means when done with genuine concern for their well-being.
