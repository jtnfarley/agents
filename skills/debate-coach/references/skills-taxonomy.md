# Argumentation Skill Bank

The fixed set of skills tracked in the progress file's `items`. Each becomes an entry like `clear_claim` when introduced. Treat the tiers below as a rough introduction order, not a rigid gate — if the learner's own situation calls for an advanced skill early (e.g. they're walking into a hostile negotiation next week and need `stay_calm` now), pull it forward.

Each entry: **id** — one-line definition — a note on what "doing it well" looks like, for judging warm-up reps and sparring debriefs.

## Foundational

- **clear_claim** — State the actual position in one unhedged sentence before anything else. Doing it well: the other person could repeat the claim back accurately after one listen; no throat-clearing ("I don't know, maybe, but...") in front of it.
- **evidence_support** — Back a claim with something concrete — a specific instance, number, or precedent — not just restating the claim louder. Doing it well: the evidence would survive the question "wait, can you give me an example?"
- **clear_ask** — End with a specific, actionable ask, not just a grievance. Doing it well: the other person knows exactly what saying "yes" would commit them to.
- **stay_calm** — Keep tone level when the exchange gets heated or personal. Doing it well: word choice and pacing don't shift even when provoked; no matching an insult with an insult.
- **ask_clarifying** — Ask a real question to understand the other side rather than assuming their position. Doing it well: the question couldn't be answered by something already said — it's seeking new information, not a rhetorical trap.

## Core

- **steelmanning** — State the opponent's position in its strongest form before responding to it. Doing it well: the opponent would say "yes, that's what I mean" to the restatement.
- **spot_fallacies_others** — Notice logical fallacies in the other side's argument (strawman, false dichotomy, slippery slope, ad hominem, appeal to emotion, hasty generalization) and name the actual gap rather than just feeling like something's off. Doing it well: names the specific leap, not just "that's not fair."
- **avoid_fallacies_self** — Don't commit those same fallacies while making your own case. Doing it well: the argument holds up if you swap in the opponent's perspective and ask "is this still fair?"
- **anticipate_counterarguments** — Raise and answer the likely rebuttal inside your own argument, before the other side gets to it. Doing it well: it doesn't feel defensive — it reads as "I've thought about this already," not "I'm bracing for attack."
- **distinguish_fact_feeling** — Separate what's verifiable from what's an emotional reaction, and label which is which rather than blending them. Doing it well: a sentence like "I feel dismissed when this happens" instead of "you always dismiss me" (the latter smuggles a feeling in as a fact).
- **concede_gracefully** — Acknowledge a specific valid point without collapsing the whole position. Doing it well: "you're right that X" followed by "and I still think Y, because [the part X doesn't actually touch]" — not a full retreat, not a grudging non-concession either.

## Advanced

- **reframe_not_dismiss** — Redirect the conversation toward what's actually resolvable without invalidating the other person's underlying concern. Doing it well: the other person feels heard even as the frame shifts — contrast with "that's not the real issue," which dismisses.
- **read_audience** — Adjust approach based on who's actually being argued with — a boss needs a different register than a partner or a stranger online. Doing it well: the same underlying point, recognizably reshaped for stakes and relationship.
- **know_when_to_disengage** — Recognize when continuing won't change anything and exit without it reading as losing. Doing it well: a clean, non-defensive exit line ("I don't think we're going to land this tonight — can we pick it up tomorrow?") rather than either storming off or grinding on past the point of use.
- **use_analogy** — Reach for a concrete analogy or example that makes an abstract point land immediately. Doing it well: the analogy actually maps — it doesn't just sound clever, it clarifies.
- **specific_language** — Prefer concrete, specific language over vague generalities ("three times this month" beats "you always"). Doing it well: every vague quantifier or absolute ("always," "never," "everyone") gets pressure-tested and usually swapped for something checkable.

## Using this bank

- Introduce 1–2 new items per session (see SKILL.md's New Material step), pulling from the earliest tier that still has un-introduced items, unless the learner's stated situation calls for something specific out of order.
- The "doing it well" notes are what a sparring or coaching debrief should actually check against — not a rubric to recite, just the bar for "did this land."
- These are also the material for **lessons mode**: one item, one short explainer, one concrete example, one quick practice rep.
